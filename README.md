# socmod-minmaj-net
The socmod implementation of the minority majority network diffusion model

## Prominent minority groups and cohesive majority groups can promote prevalent adaptation

Indegenous practices to mitigate climate risks have had at least dozens of generations to evolve to suit their native land. In the South Pacific Islands, mangrove forest management  has long been used by Indigenous peoples to mitigate inundation risk by dispersing incoming water from storm surges, tsunamis, etc. Several tribes ancestral to the Sierra Nevada mountains in North America have long used prescribed burns to mitigate catastrophic wildfire risk by strategically, safely, and ceremoniously burning overgrown parts of the forest. Unfortunately, though, it seems we continue miss opportunities for effective climate action that could potentially harness traditional climate adaptation methods. These mitigation practices (i.e., behaviors) could be useful for both minoritized Indigenous peoples and the majority group comprised of all non-Indigenous peoples because cultural evolution has tailored them to be well-suited to where mitigation is needed. Clearly, too, equity and justice call for minority groups (by numbers or marginalization) to be included in climate action decisionmaking. 

In [Turner, et al., (2023)](https://www.cakex.org/sites/default/files/documents/turner-et-al-2023-minority-group-incubators-and-majority-group-reservoirs-support-the-diffusion-of-climate-change.pdf) we showed that the relatively high prominence of minority group members means that simulated adaptation interventions were more likely to succeed when the minority group introduced the adaptation. In other words, because there are relatively few minority group members, they are more likely to be observed by at least one majority group member, compared to the inverse. That is, the average majority group member will be observed less frequently on average than a minority group member whenever there is minority-majority group structure. 

Surprisingly, we also found that relatively high, but solidly non-extreme, majority group homophily could actually _promote_ the diffusion of the model adaptation when the minority group introduces the adaptation. This _majority reservoir_ effect enables knowledge of the adaptation to accumulate in large numbers. A moderately high majority group homophily increases transitivity to protect and speed transmission of behavioral knowledge, especially as behavioral knowledge enters the majority group via observations of minority group members. If homophily were too high, there would not be enough observation of the minority group. In other words, the minority prominence effect would be suppressed due to infrequent learning from the minority by the majority.

In this paper we report the result of a series of model analyses to understand the social networks that arise in a directed social network formulation of the model dynamics we developed previously. In that work, instead of _minority prominence_ effect, we called it the _minority incubator_ effect. Through our analyses here, we explain why it is really the visibility, i.e., _prominence_, of the average minority group member compared to the average majority group member that explains the effect. Here, we develop the asymmetric directed social learning model we use to calculate the average number of majority group observers of a minority group member and vice-versa, to quantify the difference in prominence. 

We then analyze whether we recover the same patterns in adaptation diffusion success rate across systematically varied asymmetric homophily values, i.e., each group has a different homophily.
